    Your first simulation¶

    This tutorial runs the packaged mission in the desktop app, reads the main results, and then shows the one thing an inertial navigator cannot do on its own. It takes about ten minutes and needs no programming.

    You need the app installed: see Download & install. The map in step 6 also needs an internet connection, for its background tiles.

    1. Open the app¶

    Start the app: ins_sim.exe on Windows, ./ins_sim/ins_sim on Linux, or python -m ins_sim.gui if you run it from a source checkout.

    The window has two parts. The control panel on the left holds every setting. The results area on the right says Run a simulation to see results until you do.

    The first time it starts, the app creates two folders next to itself, trajectories and imu_specs, and puts a copy of the packaged mission and IMU spec in them. These are the files the two lists at the top of the control panel show.

    2. Look at the mission and the IMU¶

    Two files describe a simulation:

    • The mission (Trajectory list): bqn_departure.yaml. An 80-minute flight from Rafael Hernández Airport (BQN) at the north-west corner of Puerto Rico. The aircraft takes off, climbs to 15,000 ft and flies five circles in a holding pattern off the north coast. It then crosses the island to hold again south of it, and finally accelerates to Mach 0.8 for a sprint to the south-west: 440 nautical miles in all.
    • The IMU (IMU spec list): imu_spec.yaml. A navigation-grade unit, of the ring-laser-gyro kind that airliners carry. Its gyro biases drift by about 0.01 °/h.

    Leave both as they are.

    3. Choose quick settings¶

    The defaults, 50 trials at a 0.1 s time step, give the most precise results but take about ten minutes. For a first look, set:

    • Monte Carlo iterations: 10 trials. Each trial flies the same mission with a fresh draw of every sensor error, so ten trials show ten possible outcomes.
    • Time step (dt): 1.00 s. The navigator updates once per second instead of ten times.

    Leave Baro altitude aiding checked, and every box under Output Visualizations checked: each one becomes a tab of results.

    4. Run¶

    Click Run Simulation. The log under the button reports each step, and the progress bar fills as the trials complete. After about 15 seconds the results appear:

    5. Read the CEP tab¶

    The first tab shows how far each trial's position estimate is from the truth, horizontally, in nautical miles, over the flight's 1.3 hours. Every faint line is one trial.

    • The trials spread out because each one drew different sensor errors. Ten trials of the same IMU end anywhere from 0.3 to 3.7 nmi off.
    • The CEP, the circular error probable, is the radius that holds half of the trials. The table under the plot gives it at each whole hour: 0.90 nmi after one hour, while 95% of the trials are within 2.44 nmi.
    • The dashed line is a straight-line fit to the CEP over the first hour. Its slope, here 0.97 nmi per hour, is the usual one-number summary of an INS: this IMU makes a "one nautical mile per hour" navigator, as expected of navigation grade.

    The app always uses the same random seed, so the same settings give the same numbers every time.

    6. Look at the position errors and the map¶

    Open the Position Errors tab. It shows the North, East and Down errors over time: a dark line for the average over the trials, and a shaded band of ±3 standard deviations around it. The North and East bands widen to kilometres, but the Down band stays within metres. The barometric altimeter holds the height, as the next step shows.

    Then open the Map tab: the true track over Puerto Rico, every trial's estimated track, and a shaded band that holds 95% of the trials. White dots mark every five minutes. The band widens along the flight as the errors grow. Hover over the track to read the time and the error.

    7. Switch off the barometer¶

    An inertial navigator measures height by integrating acceleration, and that calculation is unstable. Gravity weakens with height, so a navigator that thinks it is too high computes too little gravity. It then believes it is accelerating upward, which makes its height error grow further. Aircraft use a barometric altimeter to hold this in check, and so does the simulator, unless you switch it off.

    Uncheck Baro altitude aiding, click Run Simulation again and open Position Errors:

    The Down error now grows roughly tenfold every 20 minutes: about 600 m (3σ) after 10 minutes, 35 km after 40 minutes and 2,000 km by the end. The axis labels use SI prefixes: 50k is 50 km and 2M is 2,000 km. The height error also corrupts the horizontal solution: the CEP after one hour is now 2.5 nmi instead of 0.9. The Vertical channel page explains the instability and the loop that tames it.

    Check the box again before your next run.
